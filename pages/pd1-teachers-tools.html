AI for Your Daily Work

0:00–0:05 · Warm-up

Where does AI already touch your day?

Before we build anything, take stock. You probably use AI more than you think, and naming that is the first step to using it on purpose.

Pair-share: your AI day

With a partner, take two minutes each. Where has AI already shown up in your day, at school or at home? Autocorrect, a search suggestion, a map reroute, a draft email, a recommendation. It counts. Notice how often it's already quietly helping.

Reality check: what AI can and can't do

AI is excellent at drafting, summarizing, and generating options fast. It is unreliable at facts, math, and knowing your students. It can be confidently wrong. That gap, speed on one side, judgment on the other, is exactly where this session lives.

Your job for the next 40 minutes

Keep your professional judgment in charge. AI suggests, you decide. Everything you build today is a draft you own.

0:05–0:15 · The mindset

Three habits of effective AI use

1. Tell it the role & the goal

Start with context. "I am a 9th-grade biology teacher. Help me design a 20-minute warm-up that reviews cell division." Specific beats vague, every time.

2. Treat output as a draft

AI gives you a strong first draft, never a final answer. You are the one who knows your students, your standards, and your context. Edit, verify, and adapt everything.

3. Keep your judgment in charge

AI can be confidently wrong (we call it "hallucination"). Verify facts, math, and citations. If the output feels off, it probably is. You decide. AI suggests.

The #1 habit to build: never paste student data carelessly

Before you paste anything into an AI tool, check the Privacy & Student Data rules. In short: no full names, no grades, no identifiable info in tools that aren't approved for student data. Use initials or remove names when in doubt.

0:15–0:30 · Hands-on

Build three workflows you can use tomorrow

Choose any three of the workflows below. Each one gives you a copy-ready prompt. During the session, try at least one live in the tool of your choice (see the Toolbox).

Universal output rules, built into every prompt

Every prompt below asks the AI to return a standalone, print-ready HTML document instead of markdown. That way you can open the file in any browser, print it to a PDF, or paste it straight into Canvas, Google Docs, or a class page, no extra formatting needed. The rules baked into each prompt are: no em dashes (ever), no bullet lists unless you ask for them, use headings and short sentences, use tables when the data is column-like, and style for clean letter-size printing. If a format other than HTML fits better, the prompt says so and offers the alternative.

How to turn the HTML into a PDF, the short version: when the AI returns the HTML, copy it into a new file named something like lesson.html, or in many tools choose an option to open or download it as a file. Then open that file in a browser such as Chrome or Edge. Choose File, Print. In the print window, choose "Save as PDF" as the destination and set the paper size to Letter. That gives you a clean handout you can print or send. You can also paste the HTML directly into the HTML editor or rich-text view in Canvas, Google Docs, or a class page.

Workflow: Lesson planning that respects your time

Get a structured starting point, then make it yours. AI drafts the skeleton; you bring the life.

Copy-ready prompt

"I am a [subject] teacher for [grade level]. I teach [course or class name], about [number] students. Design a complete, [length]-minute lesson on [topic].

Context to design for: [paste anything useful here, what students already know, the unit or project this fits into, materials you have, and any pacing or classroom constraints].

The lesson must include all of these parts, each under its own clear heading:
1. A hook, an opening that pulls students in and connects to what they already know.
2. The key learning target, written as a clear, student-friendly objective and aligned to this standard: [paste standard or standard code].
3. An active student task. Describe it step by step so another teacher could run it without more explanation. Make sure students are doing something, not just watching or listening.
4. Differentiation built into the task, not added on at the end, for [struggling learners] and for [advanced learners].
5. A quick formative check, so I can see in the moment who is getting it and who is not.
6. A closing and a short exit ticket.

Keep the cognitive load low, one clear idea at a time. No busywork, no fluff. Write in a friendly, practical tone I can actually use tomorrow.

Output rules, apply these to everything you produce for me:
Output one complete, standalone HTML document. Include the opening and closing tags, a <style> section, and all content inside it. Do not use markdown, code fences, or plain text outside the HTML.
Style the HTML so it prints cleanly to PDF. Use a white background, a readable font at about 11 to 12 points, comfortable line spacing, and normal margins for letter-size or A4 paper. Add print rules so a heading is never the last line on a page and a table row is never split across pages. Keep it simple and professional, no dark backgrounds or loud colors.
Use headings and short paragraphs to organize the content. When the information is naturally a set of columns, use an HTML table instead of a list.
Hard rule: never use em dashes (the long dash). Use periods or commas instead.
Hard rule: do not use bullet points or numbered lists in the output unless I specifically ask for them. Prefer clear headings, short sentences, and tables.
Write in plain, concrete language. No vague filler.
At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser, then choose File, Print, Save as PDF. You can also paste the HTML straight into Canvas, Google Docs, or a class page. If you would like a CSV planning grid version instead, ask me for one.'"

Make it yours

  • Replace the placeholders with your real topic and students.
  • Add the specific standards and resources you know you have.
  • Ask follow-ups: "Make the hook more engaging," "shorten to 30 minutes."

Workflow: One task, three levels

Differentiation is powerful but time-hungry. AI helps you create tiered versions of the same task.

Copy-ready prompt

"Here is a task my students complete: [paste the full task]. I teach [grade level] [subject], and about [number] students, some of whom read below grade level and some who are ready for deeper challenge.

Create three versions of this same task, all on the same topic and all aimed at the same core learning goal, which is: [state the learning goal]. Do not lower the standard in any version, change the path and the support, never the bar.

Version A, Support level, for students who need more scaffolding. Simplify the vocabulary, break the task into smaller steps, add sentence starters, and reduce the number of choices to make. Keep the same learning goal, just give more structure.
Version B, Core level, the standard version that most students complete.
Version C, Stretch level, for students ready for a challenge. Add deeper questions, real-world application, or open-ended extension without changing the core goal.

For each version, state clearly what stays the same and what changes, and why. Then write the three versions side by side in one HTML table, one version per column, so I can see the scaffolding differences at a glance and print one clean handout.

Output rules, apply these to everything you produce for me:
Output one complete, standalone HTML document with proper opening and closing tags and a <style> section. No markdown, no code fences.
Style it so it prints cleanly to PDF. White background, readable font at about 11 to 12 points, letter-size or A4 margins, and print rules so rows and headings are not cut across pages.
Hard rule: never use em dashes. Use periods or commas.
Hard rule: no bullet points or numbered lists unless I ask for them. Use headings, short sentences, and tables.
At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser, then choose File, Print, Save as PDF. You can also paste it into Canvas, Google Docs, or a class page.'"

Make it yours

  • Never use real student names or IEP details in the prompt.
  • Adjust the wording to match how your students show readiness.

Workflow: Rubrics that are clear and fair

Turn a vague assignment into a rubric with clear, observable success criteria.

Copy-ready prompt

"Create a clear, fair rubric for this assignment: [paste the full assignment, including the prompt]. The subject is [subject] and the grade level is [grade]. Use [number] criteria that cover the important parts of the assignment.

For each criterion, write a 'meets expectations' description that is specific and observable, so I could look at a student's work and tell, without guessing, whether it meets it. Then add a shorter note for 'exceeds' and a shorter note for 'developing.' Use language a [grade] student can understand. Avoid vague words like 'good' or 'well' and describe what the work actually looks like.

Present the rubric as one clean HTML table. Rows are the criteria, columns are the performance levels, from 'developing' to 'exceeding.' Add a blank column on the far right where I can record a student's level. Keep the table readable on one printed page if possible.

Output rules, apply these to everything you produce for me:
Output one complete, standalone HTML document with proper tags and a <style> section. No markdown, no code fences.
Style it to print cleanly to PDF. White background, readable font at about 11 to 12 points, letter-size margins, and print rules so the table does not break awkwardly across pages.
Hard rule: never use em dashes. Use periods or commas.
Hard rule: no bullet points or numbered lists unless I ask for them. Use headings, short sentences, and the table.
At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser, then choose File, Print, Save as PDF. You can also paste the table into Canvas or Google Docs. If you would prefer a CSV file to import into a spreadsheet, ask me for one.'"

Make it yours

  • Have students co-edit the rubric with you, great for ownership and clarity.
  • Check it against your school's grading expectations before using.

Workflow: Faster, more consistent feedback

AI can't know your students, but it can help you phrase feedback that is specific, kind, and actionable.

Copy-ready prompt

"I am a [subject] teacher for [grade level]. Draft kind, specific, actionable feedback for a student who [describe the strength and the area to grow, no names]. Here is the work or the situation: [paste the relevant context, the assignment, and any notes you want reflected].

Write the feedback so it opens with a genuine, specific strength, named with an example, not a generic compliment. Then state one clear area to grow, in language the student can understand and act on. Then end with one concrete next step, something the student could do tonight, not a vague 'keep trying.'
Keep it encouraging and direct, about two to four sentences of body copy. Match the tone to a respectful coach, not a judge. Do not invent strengths or issues that are not in the work I gave you.

Output rules, apply these to everything you produce for me:
Output one complete, standalone HTML document with proper tags and a <style> section. No markdown, no code fences.
Structure it as a small printable feedback slip. Put a clear heading, the student's initials or ID number in a bracketed placeholder [ ], the body copy in short paragraphs, and the next step in its own short paragraph. Style it to print cleanly to PDF on letter-size paper, with room to write nothing extra, just the slip.
Hard rule: never use em dashes. Use periods or commas.
Hard rule: no bullet points or numbered lists unless I ask for them.
At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser, then choose File, Print, Save as PDF. You can also paste the text straight into Canvas or your feedback tool.'"

Make it yours

  • Type feedback that matters; use AI to rephrase and vary it.
  • Always keep the student's identity and situation out of the prompt.
  • This is a time-saver for drafting, not a replacement for knowing your students.

Workflow: Clear parent & colleague communication

Draft a tricky email in seconds, then personalize the tone so it sounds like you.

Copy-ready prompt

"I am a [subject] teacher for [grade level]. I need to write a warm, professional email to a parent or guardian about: [topic, e.g., a positive update, a concern, a reminder, a scheduling question]. Here is the context: [paste any details you want reflected, the student's situation, the class, and the outcome you are hoping for].

Write the email so it opens with a genuine, specific positive note about the student, not a generic greeting. Then state the reason for the email clearly and kindly, in plain language, no blame. Then end with an invitation to reply or a clear next step, and a friendly closing.
Keep the whole body to about 100 to 150 words. Match the tone to a partner in the student's learning, warm, direct, and human. Do not include any student name or private information. Where you need a name or a specific detail, leave a blank [ ] so I can add it myself.

Output the email body as complete HTML you can copy into Gmail or Outlook, with a clean structure. Use the subject line as a heading, then the body in short paragraphs. No signature block, I will add my own.

Output rules, apply these to everything you produce for me:
Output one complete, standalone HTML document with proper tags and a <style> section. No markdown, no code fences. Keep the styling light so it reads like an ordinary email.
Hard rule: never use em dashes. Use periods or commas.
Hard rule: no bullet points or numbered lists unless I ask for them.
At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser to preview it, then copy the text and paste it into Gmail or Outlook. There is no need to print this one.'"

Make it yours

  • Never paste real student names or sensitive details into the tool.
  • Add your own greeting and closing so it sounds like you.
  • Re-read everything before you hit send. You're accountable for it.

Workflow: Beat the blank page

When you're stuck, use AI as an idea partner to generate options you can react to.

Copy-ready prompt

"I am a [subject] teacher for [grade level]. I need [X] ideas for [goal: a hook for a unit, a way to review, a project idea, a discussion question, a way to make a topic engaging]. Here is the context: [paste the topic, what students already know, how much time you have, and anything that did not work before].

Give me [X] diverse options, each described in one or two clear sentences. Make the options genuinely different from each other, not the same idea restated. Then tell me which three you think would be most engaging for [grade] students and explain, in one or two sentences each, why those three.

After the ideas, show me how to turn my chosen idea into action, a short plan of the first three steps to set it up.

Output rules, apply these to everything you produce for me:
Output one complete, standalone HTML document with proper tags and a <style> section. No markdown, no code fences.
Present the options as an HTML table, one option per row, with a column for the idea and a column for a short note on how to use it. Put your top three recommendations in their own short section after the table. Style it to print cleanly to PDF on letter-size paper.
Hard rule: never use em dashes. Use periods or commas.
Hard rule: no bullet points or numbered lists unless I ask for them. Use headings, short sentences, and the table.
At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser, then choose File, Print, Save as PDF, or copy the options into a planning doc.'"

Make it yours

  • Use it to get unstuck, not to copy. Pick and adapt.
  • Ask for more "out-of-the-box" options if the first set is predictable.

Workflow: A syllabus, built one question at a time

Paste this into your AI tool of choice. It interviews you, one question at a time, then drafts a complete, print-ready syllabus when you say you're done.

Copy-ready prompt

"I want you to help me write a course syllabus for my class. To do this well, you will interview me one question at a time, and only after I answer do you ask the next question. Ask only one question at a time, and wait for my answer before continuing.

Ask about everything you need to write a complete syllabus. You do not need to ask about all of these, use your judgment based on my answers, but cover the important ones: the course name, subject, and grade level; my name and how students should reach me; the course description and big-picture goal; the main topics or units in order; how the class is graded (categories and weights); the grading scale if the school uses one; late-work and make-up policy; classroom expectations and rules; the materials or supplies students need; the technology and platforms we use; any required district or school policies I must include; attendance and participation expectations; how academic honesty and AI use are handled; where to find assignments and grades; and anything else that belongs on a syllabus for my school.

Keep your questions short and plain. Do not offer suggestions yet, first gather what I tell you. Never use em dashes. Do not use bullet points or numbered lists.

After you have gathered enough, ask me one final question in your own words, asking whether I have anything else I want included or any other questions you should ask. When I tell you that I have nothing more to add (I may say 'no further questions' or 'you have everything' or anything similar), stop asking questions and build the syllabus.

Then produce the syllabus as one complete, standalone HTML document with proper opening and closing tags and a <style> section. Do not use markdown or code fences. Style it so it prints cleanly to PDF, a white background, a readable font at about 11 to 12 points, and letter-size margins, with print rules so a heading is never the last line on a page. Use clear section headings for each part of the syllabus and short paragraphs, and put anything that is naturally a set of columns in an HTML table. Use the exact facts I gave you and do not invent policies, grades, or expectations that I did not mention. Where a real detail is still missing, leave a clear blank [ ] for me to fill in rather than making one up.

Never use em dashes in the syllabus. Do not use bullet points or numbered lists unless a list is the clearest way to show something, and keep those short. At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser, then choose File, Print, Save as PDF. You can also paste the HTML into Canvas, Google Docs, or your school's syllabus form.'"

Make it yours

  • Run it fresh each term so the syllabus matches your current policies.
  • Tell it your school's exact grading scale and any required district language.
  • Re-read the whole syllabus before you share it. You're accountable for it.

Workflow: A rubric, built one question at a time

Paste this into your AI tool of choice. It interviews you about the assignment, one question at a time, then drafts a clear, fair rubric when you say you're done.

Copy-ready prompt

"I want you to help me write a rubric for an assignment. To do this well, you will interview me one question at a time, and only after I answer do you ask the next question. Ask only one question at a time, and wait for my answer before continuing.

Ask about everything you need to write a strong, usable rubric. You do not need to ask about all of these, use your judgment based on my answers, but cover the important ones: the assignment itself and what students turn in; the subject and grade level; the main learning goals or standards the assignment assesses; how many criteria the rubric should have and what they are; how many performance levels the school or I want (for example, meeting, approaching, and developing, or a 4-point scale); how strict or lenient the descriptions should be; whether students will see the rubric before they start; how the rubric connects to the grade; and anything else that will make the rubric fair and clear for my students.

Keep your questions short and plain. Do not offer the rubric yet, first gather what I tell you. Never use em dashes. Do not use bullet points or numbered lists.

After you have gathered enough, ask me one final question in your own words, asking whether I have anything else I want included or any other questions you should ask. When I tell you that I have nothing more to add (I may say 'no further questions' or 'you have everything' or anything similar), stop asking questions and build the rubric.

Then produce the rubric as one complete, standalone HTML document with proper opening and closing tags and a <style> section. Do not use markdown or code fences. Present the rubric as a clean HTML table, with one row per criterion and one column per performance level, plus a blank column on the far right where I can record a student's level. Write each level description in language a student can understand, specific and observable, not vague words like 'good' or 'well.' Style it so it prints cleanly to PDF, a white background, a readable font at about 11 to 12 points, and letter-size margins, with print rules so a table row is never split across pages and the rubric fits on as few pages as possible.

Never use em dashes in the rubric. Do not use bullet points or numbered lists. Use the exact facts I gave you and do not invent criteria, levels, or expectations that I did not mention. At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser, then choose File, Print, Save as PDF. You can also paste the table into Canvas or Google Docs. If you would prefer a CSV file to import into a spreadsheet, ask me for one.'"

Make it yours

  • Run it before you assign the work, and share the finished rubric with students up front.
  • Ask for a CSV version if you want to import the rubric into a gradebook.
  • Check the levels against your school's grading expectations before using.

Before you paste

The 10-second privacy check

Run this on every prompt before you hit send. It takes ten seconds and it protects your students.

Never put into an unapproved AI tool

Full student names · grades · IEP/504 details · medical or family information · anything identifiable.

Generally fine (with names removed)

Lesson topics · subject content · anonymized task descriptions · generic feedback phrasing · standards text.

Full details and the safe-tool list live on the Privacy & Student Data page. When in doubt, remove identifying information or don't paste it.

0:30–0:40 · Share & plan

Share one win, set your first goal

You've built three workflows. Now make them stick. This last ten minutes turns today's work into tomorrow's habit.

Share one win

With your table, share the single workflow that surprised you most, or the one you're most likely to actually use. Name the tool and the task. Hearing each other's picks is how good ideas spread.

Save your prompt toolkit

Copy your three chosen prompts into a Google Doc, a note, or a folder you can find. Add a one-line note on what to change for your subject. This is your personal prompt toolkit, and it's the thing you keep after today.

Set a "use it tomorrow" goal

Pick one concrete thing you'll do with AI in the next week. Make it specific: "I'll draft one lesson with AI before Tuesday," not "I'll use AI more." Write it down and tell a partner.

Keep the momentum going

Bring your best prompt to your next department meeting and swap it with colleagues. That's how a personal toolkit becomes a shared one.

Leave with this

  • Three workflows you built (or customized) today, saved to your prompt toolkit.
  • One "use it tomorrow" goal, e.g., "I'll draft one lesson with AI before Tuesday."
  • The habit of checking privacy before you paste.
