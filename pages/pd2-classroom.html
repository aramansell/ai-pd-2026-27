AI With Students in the Classroom

AI under your supervision can deepen learning — it can also quietly do the thinking students should be doing. In this session you'll design AI classroom activities that raise engagement and protect cognitive load, equity, and student data.

⏱️ ≈ 40 min 🎓 With students 🎒 Takeaway: 2 activities + data checklist

The 40 minutes

Agenda at a glance

0:05–0:15 · The rule

The cognitive-load test every activity must pass

Ask two questions about any AI classroom activity. If you can't answer "yes," redesign it.

❓

Q1 — Who is doing the thinking?

If the AI produces the answer and the student just copies it, the activity fails — it lowers cognitive load in the wrong way. Students must still do the important thinking.

🧠

Q2 — What is the student's job?

A good AI activity gives students a judgment task: evaluate, compare, critique, apply, revise, or decide. The AI produces raw material; the student produces the thinking.

Design moves that keep the student's brain on

🔄 Make AI the "draft," student the "editor"

AI writes a weak first draft (or a deliberately flawed one). Students improve, correct, and justify the changes.

⚖️ Use AI to create choices

AI generates three possible approaches. Students choose, defend their pick, and explain why the others fall short.

🔎 Make AI the thing being studied

Students evaluate AI output for bias or errors. AI becomes the subject, not the source.

0:15–0:30 · Hands-on

Ready-to-adapt classroom activities

These templates work across subjects. Pick two, adapt them to your grade and content, and run them through the cognitive-load test.

Activity — "Be the editor"

Students improve AI-generated work. The thinking is all theirs; AI is just the raw material.

How it works

  1. You (or AI) generate a deliberately flawed piece of work — a paragraph with errors, a weak argument, an over-generalized explanation.
  2. Students work in pairs to improve it: fix facts, tighten argument, add evidence, correct tone.
  3. Each pair explains one change and why they made it.

Subject spin

  • ELA: Improve a vague thesis or a run-on summary.
  • Science: Correct an explanation of a process that gets one step wrong.
  • History: Strengthen a claim that lacks evidence.
  • Math: Fix a worked solution with a common mistake.

Activity — "Is the AI right?"

Build critical AI literacy by making the AI the thing students investigate.

How it works

  1. Pose a question and have the AI answer it.
  2. Students fact-check the answer against a textbook, article, or their own knowledge.
  3. They rate it: accurate / partly accurate / inaccurate — and cite their evidence.

Subject spin

  • Science/Social studies: Watch for hallucinated facts, dates, and citations.
  • Math: Check the steps and the answer.
  • ELA: Judge whether the AI's tone and audience fit.
💡

This is a powerful way to teach verification — a skill students will use for life.

Activity — "Socratic partner"

AI asks questions, student answers and defends. The student does the reasoning.

How it works

  1. Set the AI up as a coach: "Ask me one question at a time to help me deepen my thinking on [topic]. Don't give answers."
  2. Students answer each question; AI asks a deeper follow-up.
  3. Students write a short reflection on how their thinking changed.

Subject spin

  • ELA: Develop a thesis through questioning.
  • Science: Probe the reasoning behind a hypothesis.
  • Math: Walk through a problem, with AI asking about each step.
⚠️

Test the prompt yourself first. Some AIs jump to giving answers — add "don't give the answer" and model it.

Activity — "Three options, you decide"

AI offers choices; students make a real decision and defend it.

How it works

  1. Ask the AI for three distinct approaches / explanations / solutions to a question.
  2. Students pick the strongest and defend their choice against the others.
  3. Class discusses what made the winner strong.

Subject spin

  • Social studies: Three possible causes of an event — which is most important?
  • Math: Three solution methods — which is most efficient?
  • Science: Three experimental designs — which would you run?

Activity — "Explain it to me like I'm..."

Students learn by asking for a clearer explanation — then teaching it back.

How it works

  1. A student asks the AI to explain a concept at their level.
  2. The student then explains it back to a partner without the AI.
  3. Partner checks the explanation; together they note what the AI missed or got wrong.

Subject spin

  • Great for vocabulary, procedures, and any "aha, now I get it" moment.
  • Encourage students to ask for an analogy or a real-world example.

Before students log in

The student data-safety checklist

Before any AI activity, run this checklist. Full rules on the Privacy page.

✅
Allowed

Anonymized, topic-level content. Pseudonyms. Class projects with no identifying info. Tools approved by the school for student use.

🚫
Not allowed in unapproved tools

Student names, grades, photos, voice, addresses, IEP/504 details, or any personally identifiable information (PII).

  1. Check the tool

    Is it on the school's approved-for-student list? See Free vs Paid Plans and the Toolbox.

  2. Use shared class accounts where appropriate

    For younger students or where sign-in is a barrier, a teacher-run class account keeps students anonymous.

  3. No personal details in prompts

    Teach students to use pseudonyms and to never type their name, school, or location.

  4. Model it out loud

    Think aloud while writing a prompt: "I'm not going to type anyone's name — just the topic."

  5. Review output before it's shared

    Students should not post AI output publicly (or on a class site) until it's checked.

Bias, race & inclusion

Keep equity at the center

⚖️ Equal access

Prefer free tools and ones that work on Chromebooks so every student can participate, at school and at home.

🌍 Name the bias

AI reflects the data it was trained on. Build activities where students look for bias — in representation, language, and example choices.

🙋 Include every voice

AI can over-represent confident voices. Structure activities so every student responds (pair-then-share, written-first) before AI discussion.

💡

English learners and students with learning differences often benefit most from AI as a support — text-to-anything, translation, sentence starters. Design with them in mind, not as an afterthought.
