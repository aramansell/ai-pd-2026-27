SESSION 2 · PD SESSION

AI With Students in the Classroom

AI under your supervision can deepen learning, but it can also quietly do the thinking students should be doing. In this session you'll design AI classroom activities that raise engagement and protect cognitive load, equity, and student data.

0:00–0:05 · Warm-up

"AI did my homework"

Start with honesty. Every teacher in this room has a fear or a hope about students and AI. Naming them out loud is the first step to a shared, workable approach.

Name the fears

With a partner, take two minutes. What worries you most about students using AI? Plagiarism, lost thinking, unfair advantage, students who won't try. Write them down. There are no wrong answers here, this is the honest starting point.

Name the hopes

Now flip it. What could students do better with AI in their hands? A stuck writer gets unstuck, a shy student gets a practice partner, a curious one goes deeper. Hold both lists. This session is about the hopes without ignoring the fears.

The question we're answering today

What do we want students to do with AI? Not "should they use it," but "how do we make sure they use it well, under our direction." That's the whole session.

0:05–0:15 · The rule

The cognitive-load test every activity must pass

Ask two questions about any AI classroom activity. If you can't answer "yes," redesign it.

Q1: Who is doing the thinking?

If the AI produces the answer and the student just copies it, the activity fails, it lowers cognitive load in the wrong way. Students must still do the important thinking.

Q2: What is the student's job?

A good AI activity gives students a judgment task: evaluate, compare, critique, apply, revise, or decide. The AI produces raw material; the student produces the thinking.

Design moves that keep the student's brain on

Make AI the "draft," student the "editor"

AI writes a weak first draft (or a deliberately flawed one). Students improve, correct, and justify the changes.

Use AI to create choices

AI generates three possible approaches. Students choose, defend their pick, and explain why the others fall short.

Make AI the thing being studied

Students evaluate AI output for bias or errors. AI becomes the subject, not the source.

The trap to avoid: our AI working with their AI

If we ask the AI to make a 10-question quiz, students can simply ask the same AI to complete it for them. That's our AI working with their AI, nobody does the thinking. Before you assign any AI activity, ask: "Could a student just hand this exact task to the AI and get a passing result?" If yes, redesign it. The best activities give the student a thinking job the AI can't do for them, reflecting on their own experience, defending their own reasoning, or interviewing someone in a role.

0:15–0:30 · Hands-on

Ready-to-adapt classroom activities

These templates work across subjects. Pick two, adapt them to your grade and content, and run them through the cognitive-load test.

Activity, "Be the editor"

Students improve AI-generated work. The thinking is all theirs; AI is just the raw material.

How it works

  1. You (or AI) generate a deliberately flawed piece of work, a paragraph with errors, a weak argument, an over-generalized explanation.
  2. Students work in pairs to improve it: fix facts, tighten argument, add evidence, correct tone.
  3. Each pair explains one change and why they made it.

Subject spin

  • ELA: Improve a vague thesis or a run-on summary.
  • Science: Correct an explanation of a process that gets one step wrong.
  • History: Strengthen a claim that lacks evidence.
  • Math: Fix a worked solution with a common mistake.

Activity, "Is the AI right?"

Build critical AI literacy by making the AI the thing students investigate.

How it works

  1. Pose a question and have the AI answer it.
  2. Students fact-check the answer against a textbook, article, or their own knowledge.
  3. They rate it: accurate / partly accurate / inaccurate, and cite their evidence.

Subject spin

  • Science/Social studies: Watch for hallucinated facts, dates, and citations.
  • Math: Check the steps and the answer.
  • ELA: Judge whether the AI's tone and audience fit.

This is a powerful way to teach verification, a skill students will use for life.

Activity, "AI interviews the student"

The AI becomes an interactive reflection partner that asks questions tailored to each student's own project and experience, not one pre-defined set for the whole class.

Why this works

  • The cognitive load stays on the student, they do the reflecting, explaining, and articulating.
  • Engagement is higher because the AI adapts to what the individual student actually did and said.
  • The AI's follow-up questions are custom to that student's experience, so the activity can't be pre-answered or copied.

How it works

  1. Set the AI up as an interviewer/reflection coach (see the prompt template below).
  2. Students answer the AI's questions about their own project, draft, or process.
  3. Because the questions follow their answers, each student's reflection is unique.
  4. Students turn the interview into a written reflection or use it to plan their next step.

Subject spin

  • Any project: "Interview me about my project so I can reflect on what I learned and what I'd do differently."
  • Writing: "Ask me about my essay's argument, one question at a time, so I can sharpen my thesis."
  • Science: "Ask me about my hypothesis and my procedure so I can see where my reasoning might be weak."
  • Lab/fieldwork: Turn it into a "debrief" that draws out the student's own observations.
Copy-ready prompt (for students)

"You are an interviewer and reflection coach. Ask me one question at a time about my [project / essay / lab / writing]. Your questions must follow from what I say, dig into my thinking, ask why, and push me to be specific. Do not give me answers and do not do the work for me. Keep each question short and answerable in a few sentences. After about 6 questions, summarize in a few sentences what you noticed about my thinking, and suggest one question for me to think about next. Never use em dashes. Do not use bullet points or numbered lists, just ask your questions in plain sentences."

Activity, "Role-play interview"

The AI plays a role and the learner interviews it. The student drives the questioning, so the thinking is theirs.

How it works

  1. Set the AI up to play a character or person with specific background (see the prompt template below).
  2. Students interview that character to gather information, test a hypothesis, or solve a problem.
  3. Students must ask good follow-up questions to get what they need. The AI stays in character and doesn't just hand over answers.
  4. Students synthesize what they learned into a write-up, decision, or next question.

Subject spin

  • History: Interview a historical figure or an eyewitness to an event.
  • Science: Interview an expert witness or a patient (as in clinical reasoning), then build a diagnosis or decision.
  • ELA: Interview a character from a novel to understand their motivations.
  • Economics/Civics: Interview a policymaker or a business owner facing a dilemma.
Copy-ready prompt (for students)

"You are [character/role] with this background: [one or two sentences]. Stay in character the whole time. I am going to interview you to [goal]. Answer only based on the background you were given, if I ask something you do not know, say so rather than making it up. Do not volunteer extra information unless I ask. Keep your answers short and in character. Never use em dashes. Do not use bullet points or numbered lists, just answer in plain sentences."

Activity, "Socratic seminar"

The AI guides the learner through a Socratic-seminar-style lesson, asking questions that push their thinking deeper.

How it works

  1. Set the AI up as a Socratic guide on a topic: "Ask me one question at a time, don't give answers."
  2. Students answer each question; the AI asks a deeper follow-up based on their answer.
  3. Students write a short reflection on how their thinking changed.

Subject spin

  • ELA: Develop a thesis through questioning.
  • Science: Probe the reasoning behind a hypothesis.
  • Math: Walk through a problem, with AI asking about each step.

Test the prompt yourself first. Some AIs jump to giving answers, add "don't give the answer" and model it.

Activity: "Three options, you decide"

AI offers choices; students make a real decision and defend it.

How it works

  1. Ask the AI for three distinct approaches / explanations / solutions to a question.
  2. Students pick the strongest and defend their choice against the others.
  3. Class discusses what made the winner strong.

Subject spin

  • Social studies: Three possible causes of an event, which is most important?
  • Math: Three solution methods, which is most efficient?
  • Science: Three experimental designs, which would you run?

Activity, "Explain it to me like I'm..."

Students learn by asking for a clearer explanation, then teaching it back.

How it works

  1. A student asks the AI to explain a concept at their level.
  2. The student then explains it back to a partner without the AI.
  3. Partner checks the explanation; together they note what the AI missed or got wrong.

Subject spin

  • Great for vocabulary, procedures, and any "aha, now I get it" moment.
  • Encourage students to ask for an analogy or a real-world example.

0:25–0:30 · Brainstorm your own

Turn any task into an AI-thinking activity

The activity templates above are starting points. Use this short process to turn any existing lesson or task into a high-cognitive-load AI activity, and to write prompts that get you there.

Frame the prompt from your role, not the AI's

Instead of telling the AI "you are an expert 11th grade life science teacher, help me…", frame it as "I am an 11th grade life science teacher. Help me…" It's a small change that keeps you, and your professional judgment, as the expert in the conversation, with the AI as the assistant.

Three moves to make any task "AI-proof" for thinking

1. Make the student the source

Any task where the answer depends on the student's own work, experience, or reasoning can't be copied from AI. Turn "write a reflection on photosynthesis" into "the AI interviews you about your own lab, then you turn it into a reflection."

2. Make the AI the role, not the answer

Have the AI play someone, a witness, a patient, a character, a policymaker, and let the student interview it. The student drives the questioning, so they do the thinking.

3. Make the AI a guide, not a producer

Set the AI to ask questions one at a time (Socratic), pushing the student to explain, defend, and deepen. The AI never hands over the final answer. It coaches the student toward their own.

A prompt-brainstorming routine for teachers

  1. Pick a task you already assign

    Bring a lesson, worksheet, or project prompt you use today.

  2. Ask the AI for three "thinking activity" versions

    Use a framing like: "I am an 11th grade life science teacher. Here's a task I assign: [task]. Give me three ways to turn this into an activity where the AI interviews or guides the student, so the cognitive load stays on the student, not on AI producing the answer. For each, give me the student-facing AI prompt and note exactly what thinking job the student must do that the AI can't do for them."

  3. Run each through the "their AI" test

    For each version, ask: "Could a student hand this whole thing to the AI and pass without thinking?" If yes, change it so the student must draw on their own work, experience, or reasoning.

  4. Pick one and test it yourself

    Play the student and run the prompt. Adjust the role, the constraints, and the "don't give answers" language until the student is clearly doing the thinking.

Copy-ready brainstorming prompt

"I am a [subject] teacher for [grade level]. I teach about [number] students. Here is a task I currently assign: [paste the full task]. Turn it into an AI-supported classroom activity that keeps the cognitive load on the student and raises engagement.

Three options that work well are: the AI interviews the student about their own work, the AI plays a role the student interviews, or the AI guides a Socratic-style lesson. Give me all three options for this task. For each option, give me the student-facing AI prompt, the exact setup you would use, and a note on what thinking job the student must do that the AI cannot do for them.

Then tell me which option you recommend for [grade] and why, and describe how the student's finished work should be turned in so I can grade the thinking, not the AI's output.

Output rules, apply these to everything you produce for me:
Output one complete, standalone HTML document with proper tags and a <style> section. No markdown, no code fences.
Present the three options one after another under clear headings, with the student-facing prompt for each in a distinct block I can copy. Use short sentences, no filler. Style it to print cleanly to PDF on letter-size paper.
Hard rule: never use em dashes. Use periods or commas.
Hard rule: no bullet points or numbered lists unless I ask for them.
At the very end, in plain text just before the HTML, add one line telling me how to use the result. Use the words: 'Open this HTML file in your browser, then choose File, Print, Save as PDF. You can also copy any single student-facing prompt from the HTML and paste it into your tool.'"

Before students log in

The student data-safety checklist

Before any AI activity, run this checklist. Full rules on the Privacy page.

Allowed

Anonymized, topic-level content. Pseudonyms. Class projects with no identifying info. Tools approved by the school for student use.

Not allowed in unapproved tools

Student names, grades, photos, voice, addresses, IEP/504 details, or any personally identifiable information (PII).

  1. Check the tool

    Is it on the school's approved-for-student list? See Free vs Paid Plans and the Toolbox.

  2. Use shared class accounts where appropriate

    For younger students or where sign-in is a barrier, a teacher-run class account keeps students anonymous.

  3. No personal details in prompts

    Teach students to use pseudonyms and to never type their name, school, or location.

  4. Model it out loud

    Think aloud while writing a prompt: "I'm not going to type anyone's name, just the topic."

  5. Review output before it's shared

    Students should not post AI output publicly (or on a class site) until it's checked.

Bias, race & inclusion

Keep equity at the center

Equal access

Prefer free tools and ones that work on Chromebooks so every student can participate, at school and at home.

Name the bias

AI reflects the data it was trained on. Build activities where students look for bias, in representation, language, and example choices.

Include every voice

AI can over-represent confident voices. Structure activities so every student responds (pair-then-share, written-first) before AI discussion.

English learners and students with learning differences often benefit most from AI as a support, text-to-anything, translation, sentence starters. Design with them in mind, not as an afterthought.

0:30–0:40 · Share & plan

Share one activity, set a date

You've designed two classroom activities. Now make them real. This last ten minutes turns today's plans into next week's lessons.

Share one activity

With your table, walk through the activity you're most excited to run. Name the subject, the grade, and the thinking job the student does. Get one concrete suggestion from a colleague to make it stronger.

Run the data-safety check

For the activity you'll actually run, walk the data-safety checklist out loud. Which tool? Is it approved for student use? What stays out of the prompt? Make sure the answer is clean before you leave.

Set a date on the calendar

Pick the week you'll run it and write it down. Tell a partner. A date on the calendar is what turns a good idea into something students actually experience.

Keep the momentum going

Bring your adapted activity to your next department meeting and trade feedback. That's how a good activity becomes a great, subject-ready one.

Leave with this

  • Two classroom activities you adapted for your subject and grade.
  • The cognitive-load test (Q1: who's thinking? Q2: what's the student's job?) to vet future activities.
  • The data-safety checklist, printed or saved.
  • One activity you'll actually run, with a date on the calendar.
