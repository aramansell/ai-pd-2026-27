0:00–0:05 · Warm-up

What do our students already do with AI?

Before we teach students anything, we need an honest picture of what they're already doing. No judgment, just facts. This is the ground truth our teaching builds on.

Take an honest inventory

With a partner, share what you've actually seen students do with AI, in your class or anywhere. A draft, a translation, a quick answer, a study tool, something you didn't expect. What you've seen is the real starting point, not what the news says.

Name what they're good at, and where they struggle

Students are often fast with AI but shallow with it. They can get an answer in seconds, but they may not know how to ask a good question, check if it's right, or use it honestly. That gap, speed without judgment, is exactly what this session teaches.

The stance that works

Students will use AI with or without us. Our job isn't to police it, it's to make sure they use it well. That's the shift this whole session is built on.

0:05–0:15 · The skills

The four AI-literacy skills every student needs

These four skills, adapted from UNESCO's AI competency framework and Common Sense Media, are the foundation of being a capable, ethical AI user.

1. Prompt craft

Students learn to ask clearly and specifically: give the AI a role, a goal, and constraints. A good prompt gets a useful answer; a vague prompt gets a generic one.

2. Critical evaluation

Students learn AI can be confidently wrong. They verify facts, check for hallucinations and fake citations, and treat every output as a draft to be examined, not a truth to be copied.

3. Ethics & academic honesty

Students learn when using AI is allowed, when it isn't, and how to be transparent about their use. We teach them the difference between using a tool and passing off work that isn't theirs.

4. Bias awareness

Students learn AI reflects its training data and can carry bias about race, gender, and culture. They learn to spot and question it, and to think about who is represented and who is left out.

0:15–0:30 · Hands-on

Build a ready-to-teach AI-literacy mini-lesson

Use this template to build a 20-minute lesson you can teach to your own students next week. Then draft your classroom agreement.

The "Ask, Check, Decide" lesson template

A simple, repeatable 3-step process students can use every time they touch AI.

  1. ASK: Write a good prompt

    Teach the formula: Role + Task + Details + Limits. A strong prompt tells the AI who it is, what to make, what to include, and what not to do. Example: "You are a patient study partner for a [grade] student. Help me outline an essay on [topic]. Include three body-paragraph ideas, each with one clear claim. Write in plain sentences, no bullet points, and do not write the essay for me. Never use em dashes."
    Add a small detail to make it stick: a vague prompt like "help me with my essay" gets a generic answer, while "outline an essay with three body-paragraph claims on this topic" gets something usable.

  2. CHECK: Evaluate the answer

    Teach students to ask: Does this make sense? Where would I verify this? Did it give a fake citation? Cross-check with a trusted source. Treat it as a draft.

  3. DECIDE: Use it honestly

    Teach students to decide: Am I allowed to use AI here? Do I need to tell the teacher? Am I doing the important thinking, or letting AI do it? If unsure, ask.

The "Honest Work" classroom agreement

Co-create this with your students in 10 minutes. A template is ready on the Policies page, here's the shape:

  • Be transparent: I'll tell my teacher when and how I used AI.
  • Do the thinking: I'll use AI as an assistant, never to do my thinking for me.
  • Verify: I'll check AI output against trustworthy sources.
  • Protect privacy: I'll never type my name, school, or personal info into AI.
  • When in doubt, ask: If I'm not sure AI use is allowed, I'll ask my teacher.

The honest-work conversation

Moving from "caught you" to "we teach you"

The most effective approach to academic honesty with AI is to define allowed use clearly and teach it, not to rely on detection (detectors are unreliable and can be biased).

Usually allowed (check your policy)

Brainstorming, getting feedback on your own draft, checking grammar, asking for a clearer explanation, organizing notes.

Usually not allowed (check your policy)

Copying AI output as your own, using it to answer a graded assessment, passing off AI work as your writing.

The key shift

Students don't need to be tricked into honesty. They need clear expectations and the skills to meet them. When every teacher defines allowed use, students stop guessing.

PPS "Levels of Use"

PPS defines three levels for student AI use: Restrictive (AI not allowed), Moderate (AI allowed with citation), and Permissive (AI allowed without citation). State which level applies to each assignment. See the Policies page for the full definitions.

Address misuse restoratively

When AI use is inappropriate, PPS guidance is a restorative conversation: explore the harm, co-create solutions, and re-educate the student on responsible use, not a punitive "gotcha." Don't rely on AI detection tools (they're unreliable and biased against non-native English speakers).

Bias, race & inclusion

A bias lesson you can run in 15 minutes

"Who's in the picture?"

  1. Have the AI generate a list of people "in a profession", e.g., "name famous scientists," "who is a great leader," "name successful [field] workers."
  2. Students look for patterns: who's represented? Who's missing? What assumptions does the AI make?
  3. Discuss: Where does this bias come from? (Training data, the AI mirrors the world it learned from.)
  4. Extend: Ask the AI the same question with different framing and compare results.

This works across subjects, from history to science to careers. It turns AI into a mirror for examining bias, which is exactly the skill students need.

0:30–0:40 · Share & plan

Share one idea, plan your first lesson

You've built a mini-lesson and a class agreement. Now make them real. This last ten minutes turns today's drafts into next week's teaching.

Share one idea

With your table, share the one thing you'll take back to your classroom, a lesson, an agreement line, a bias activity. Name the subject and the grade. Get one suggestion from a colleague to make it stronger.

Plan your first AI-literacy lesson

Pick the week you'll run your "Ask, Check, Decide" mini-lesson and write it down. Tell a partner. A date on the calendar is what turns a good idea into something students actually experience.

Draft your class agreement

Take the "Honest Work" template and write the first two or three lines you'll co-create with your students. Keep it simple enough that a student can explain each line in their own words.

Keep the momentum going

Bring your mini-lesson and agreement to your next department meeting and align on a shared standard. That's how AI literacy becomes consistent across all your classes.
