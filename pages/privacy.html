PRIVACY & STUDENT DATA

The plain-language rules for protecting student data

When you paste something into an AI tool, where does it go? Who can see it? Does it train a model? This page gives you simple, actionable rules, no legalese.

FERPA basics What never to paste What's safe

The one rule to remember

If you wouldn't put it on the hallway bulletin board, don't put it in an AI tool

Safe to paste (with names removed)

Lesson topics · subject content · anonymized assignment prompts · standards text · generic feedback phrasing · a rubric. The moment you remove names and identifiers, the risk drops dramatically.

Never paste into an unapproved tool

Full student names · grades · IEP/504 details · medical, family, or discipline info · photos · voice recordings · email addresses · student ID numbers · anything that identifies a student.

The laws in plain English

What FERPA, COPPA, CIPA & OSIPA mean for your AI use

Several laws protect student data. The PPS AI Guidebook names all of them, here's what each means when using AI:

FERPA

The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act protects students' education records, grades, transcripts, IEPs, discipline records, anything that identifies a student. Putting these into an AI tool that isn't approved can be a FERPA problem.

COPPA

The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act requires parental consent before collecting the personal information of children under 13. Many consumer AI tools are 13+ or 18+ for this reason.

CIPA

The Children's Internet Protection Act requires filtering and monitoring of internet access for minors on school networks, which is why school Chromebooks filter content.

OSIPA (Oregon)

The Oregon Student Information Protection Act is the state law on top of the federal ones. It prohibits using student data for targeted advertising or non-educational profiling. Some AI vendors aren't aware of OSIPA, only district-approved tools are vetted for it.

The bottom line

Only use district-approved AI tools that have been vetted for compliance with FERPA, COPPA, CIPA, and OSIPA, with a Data Privacy Agreement (DPA) in place. This is why we point you to the approved tools rather than consumer free tools.

The big question

"Does this tool train on my data?"

This is the most important distinction. The answer varies by tool and by plan (free vs paid). See the Free vs Paid Plans page for the full breakdown. In general:

Situation Typical default What it means
Free consumer AI (no account / personal account) Often trains Your conversations may be used to train the model. Do not put student data here.
Paid consumer plans Usually doesn't train Most paid plans state they don't train on your conversations. Still avoid identifiable student data unless approved.
School / education plans Doesn't train Contractually protected. This is the safe way to use AI with student data.
Local AI (runs on your device) Stays on device Ollama and similar run entirely on your machine. Data never leaves your device.

Media manipulation

Deepfakes & manipulated media

The PPS AI Guidebook calls out AI's ability to create realistic fake images, video, and audio. Creating or sharing manipulated media that misrepresents, harasses, bullies, or harms someone is a prohibited use under PPS policy.

Don't do this

Creating or sharing deepfakes or AI-generated media to deceive, harass, intimidate, bully, defraud, or harm others. This includes realistic fake images, video, and audio.

Teach students to question

Build media literacy: question whether media is real, verify sources, and understand that AI can fabricate convincing content. See Session 3 for bias and evaluation lessons.

If a student creates or shares manipulated media targeting someone, follow PPS restorative practices and the Student Rights, Responsibilities, and Discipline Handbook. Report harmful or suspicious online activity per district policy.

Environmental impact

AI has a real environmental cost

The PPS AI Guidebook (Section 07) highlights that AI data centers use significant energy and water. Training a single large model can emit as much CO₂ as five gasoline-powered cars over their full lifespan.

Sustainable, thoughtful use

Before generating, ask: "Is there a way to accomplish this task with less harm?" Not every query needs a heavyweight model, simple tasks can use efficient ones.

A teaching moment

Engage students in critical conversations about AI's energy and water use, climate justice, and how technology revolutions shape resource use.

Note: per the Guidebook, Gemini is currently one of the more energy-efficient models per query, while "reasoning" models are far more energy-intensive. This matters for everyday use.

Before you paste

The 10-second decision

  1. Is there identifiable student info?

    Names, grades, IEP details, anything personal? If yes, remove it first.

  2. Is the tool approved for student data?

    Check the approved list. If it's a school/district account or a local tool, you're generally safe.

  3. Is the free tier going to train on it?

    If it's a free consumer account and might train, don't paste student data, period.

  4. When in doubt, ask

    Ask your department lead or IT before trying something new. Better to ask than to put a student at risk.

Equity & access

Privacy is also an equity issue

If some students' parents pay for premium AI and others can't, that's an equity gap. And every family has a right to know how their child's data is (and isn't) used.

Communicate with families

Let families know which AI tools you use with students, what data (if any) is involved, and how to opt out.

Prefer free, approved tools

Using free, district-approved tools means every student can participate regardless of what their family can afford.
